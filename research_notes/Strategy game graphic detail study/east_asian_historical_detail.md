## 1. Black Myth: Wukong — what its look is made of at detail level, what Game Science has said, and what has happened through November 2026

### Takeaway
The game's detail vocabulary is built on scanned real heritage (temples, clay and stone statues, rocks, forests — 36 sites, 27 of them in Shanxi) pushed through UE5 Nanite/Lumen, with hand-tuned lighting that critics rated above its own path-traced mode; Game Science has said very little in public about the pipeline itself. Since launch the studio shelved the Wukong DLC (Aug 2025) for Black Myth: Zhong Kui, whose 15-minute August 2026 trailer shows a markedly greyer, drabber palette; no release date.

### Cited Findings

Heritage scanning and sites
- [PRESS] A China News report states that the historical buildings and sculptures in the game were made by the team travelling around China to scan historical architecture, then modelling/processing the scans for the game; this is the reporter's paraphrase, not a direct quote. — [China News, 22 Aug 2024](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/cul/2024/08-22/10272777.shtml)
- [DEV] Art director Yang Qi (quoted from an earlier China Academy of Art talk): "设计非常重要，方圆结合，我们可以决定如何把现代和古典的元素进行变化与延伸" (design matters; combine square and round; decide how modern and classical elements are varied and extended); and "传统文化就在那里，看一眼你就会喜欢". — [China News, 22 Aug 2024](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/cul/2024/08-22/10272777.shtml)
- [PRESS] The game recreates 36 Chinese landmarks; the report says scanning used cameras, lidar and other sensors with "AI optical space positioning algorithms", and that scanned subjects included temples, Buddha statues, forests and rocks. Named examples: Jade Emperor (Yuhuang) Temple in Shanxi and Kaiyuan Temple in Fujian. No Game Science staff are quoted and the article does not say whether scanning was in-house. — [21st Century Business Herald, 8 Sep 2024](https://m.21jingji.com/article/20240908/herald/ef6d0bc54aa528204297d7bd0ec91d88.html)
- [PRESS] 27 of the game's 36 key visual locations are in Shanxi (Foguang Temple, Yingxian Wooden Pagoda, Xuankong/Hanging Temple named). — [China Daily, 11 Nov 2025](https://regional.chinadaily.com.cn/shanxi/2025-11/11/c_1139460.htm)
- [DEV] Feng Ji (founder, game director), in the preface to the Shanxi exhibition, says players were struck by the "authentic depictions of ancient Chinese architecture and cultural relics"; co-founder Yu Ranxing says the digital recreations of heritage sites renewed interest in traditional culture. Neither describes method. — [China Daily, 11 Nov 2025](https://regional.chinadaily.com.cn/shanxi/2025-11/11/c_1139460.htm)
- [PRESS] The in-game deities are described as "faithful recreations" of the Yuan-dynasty painted clay Twenty-Eight Mansions statues at Yuhuang Temple, Jincheng, which the developers "painstakingly scanned"; the piece highlights fierce expressions and flowing robes as what carried over. — [CGTN, 2 Mar 2026](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-03-02/From-digital-pixels-to-700-year-old-clay-1LbdSsBXw7m/share_amp.html)
- [PRESS] (snippet) Other named real sources: Dule Temple (Tianjin), Dazu Rock Carvings (Chongqing); players also identified Shisi Temple's main hall in Lishui (Southern Song, 1140), stone carvings of Nanshan Temple in Xinzhou, and the sutra pillar in front of Foguang Temple's East Hall. — [Our China Story](https://www.ourchinastory.com/cn/12623); [China News](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/cul/2024/08-22/10272777.shtml)
- [VENDOR] A scanner maker's write-up describes the generic workflow of importing scanned stone carvings and Buddha statues into ZBrush for fine sculpting; it does not confirm this was Game Science's pipeline. — [Shining3D](https://www.shining3d.com/how-black-myth-wukong-turned-chinas-historical-and-cultural-heritage-into-realistic-game-digital-models-through-3d-scanning-technology)
- [VENDOR] Photogrammetry depends on surface "feature points", which is why statues and dusty artefacts scan well. — [KIRI Engine blog](https://kiriengine.app/blog/insight/aaa-games-photogrammetry-realism)

### Inferences
- The publicly documented "detail recipe" is: real, old, weathered surfaces captured rather than invented (painted clay, carved stone, timber halls), then lit by hand. For a procedural pipeline with no scans, the transferable part is the *subject matter* of the detail (statue faces, robe folds, tile and bracket rhythm, rock) and the fact that hand-authored lighting outperformed the physically "correct" path-traced mode in at least one critic's eyes.
- Game Science's source architecture is Liao/Song/Yuan/Ming (Dule Temple, Foguang, Yingxian, Yuhuang). Copying that look into a Han-period game would import post-Han brackets, roof curvature and statuary. The method (go to the period's surviving evidence) transfers; the specific assets do not.
- Zhong Kui (Aug 2026) suggests the studio's "dark myth" palette is moving further toward low-chroma grey with sparse vegetation, which is close to the game's own cold, drained default.

## 2. Other 2024–2026 titles — which details drew praise or criticism, and what developers said

### Takeaway
Across these games, praise clusters on environments, weather-and-wind systems and dense "hero" cities; criticism clusters on performance in dense areas, clashing styles, repeated environments, and muddy or illegible dark scenes. Almost no developer has spoken publicly about Han-period armour or architecture research: the Three Kingdoms titles (Dynasty Warriors: Origins, Wo Long 2, Chinese SLGs) market spectacle and scale, not material accuracy.

### Cited Findings

Ghost of Yōtei (Sucker Punch, Oct 2025) — the best-documented detail systems
- [DEV] Adrian Bentley (director of programming): because 17th-century Hokkaido has few mirror-like surfaces, ray tracing went to global illumination, not reflections; lighting is an improved baked model plus short-range RTGI. — [PlayStation Blog tech deep dive](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Fog: local fog volumes; a light-space cloud shadow map gives god rays in volumetric fog far from camera; artists place "god ray targets" so chosen areas are more often lit through holes in cloud. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Clouds can render in front of geometry (to hide Mt. Yōtei); three cloud frames blended; faster cloud motion signals "stormy, unsettled weather". — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Snow: terrain tessellation with a runtime displacement buffer written by characters; snow knocked off trees/bushes spawns particles; a snow sparkle effect uses stable screen-space noise. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Vegetation and wind: grass and small plants deform with wind and characters; a "cut buffer" lets weapon sweeps cut grass and flowers; flower fields generated at runtime; the stated goal is that everything moves with the wind, including clothing, weapon tassels and cloth in settlements. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Particles sample terrain material, deformation and water flow; wetness, blood, mud and snow on characters come from a directional grid deformed around the character. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Distance: over one million distant trees/rocks/bushes culled to about 60,000 drawn; far mountains are baked models with terrain materials in textures. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Art director Joanna Wang on vegetation: "You feel a little bit of emotion in even a single leaf… when we repeat that in a very large field, the feeling is amplified"; the style, drawn from woodblock printing, exaggerates plant shapes (curving stalks and leaves — reporter's paraphrase). — [Atmos](https://atmos.earth/art-and-culture/this-years-most-anticipated-video-game-is-an-homage-and-elegy-to-nature/)
- [DEV] Wang, headline quote: "When the wind blows, the trees know how strong the wind is"; she wanted a "living painting". — [GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/open-world/ghost-of-yoteis-art-director-wanted-the-rpg-to-look-like-a-living-painting-but-the-detail-required-stresses-even-me-out-when-the-wind-blows-the-trees-know-how-strong-the-wind-is)
- [DEV] Wang on specifics: "we made maple [trees] still holding their leaves and not letting go"; "thick, short bamboo field" seen at Shiretoko; deep snow "when you're barely able to walk through and leave a long track". — [MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2025/09/25/ghost-of-yotei-sucker-punch-joanna-wang-art-director-interview/)

Assassin's Creed Shadows (Ubisoft, Mar 2025)
- [DEV] Technology director Pierre Fortin: the series had some global illumination for years, but ray-traced GI "allows us to be much more dynamic" — needed because lights can be switched and bamboo, stalls and fences are destructible; "micro polygon" assets adapt to visibility and distance. — [Ubisoft: Building the next generation of Assassin's Creed](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/2NvGq2f2HRuu11c03KJ0DG/assassins-creed-shadows-building-the-next-generation-of-assassins-creed)
- [DEV] Dynamic wind and spray with up to 10,000 particles on screen following the wind flow; weather muffles footsteps and darkness reduces detection. — [Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/2NvGq2f2HRuu11c03KJ0DG/assassins-creed-shadows-building-the-next-generation-of-assassins-creed)
- [DEV] Art director Thierry Dansereau: seasons change vegetation (tall grass and leafy bushes in spring, bare in winter; ponds freeze); seasonal effects kept mostly to animation level so they are not felt as negative. — [GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/assassin-s-creed/each-of-assassins-creed-shadows-four-seasons-features-different-kinds-of-weather-but-that-wont-affect-gameplay-because-ubisoft-doesnt-want-it-to-have-a-negative-impact); [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/gaming/consoles-pc/assassins-creed-shadows-open-world-will-feature-seasons-that-impact-combat-and-traversal)

- [REVIEW] Rise of the Ronin is grouped with Team NINJA titles whose reputation was marred by underwhelming visuals and launch performance. — [Wccftech Nioh 3 review](https://wccftech.com/review/nioh-3/)

### Inferences
- Systems that reviewers and developers both foreground are *motion* details, not static ones: wind through grass and cloth, snow shed from branches, cloud speed, particles following flow. For a low-asset procedural game these are cheaper to add than surface detail and are what the benchmark titles themselves treat as identity.
- The common failure modes are instructive for a dark art direction: muddy darkness against over-bright effects (Nioh series), environment geometry blocking the hero moment (Phantom Blade Zero), two unblended styles (Wuchang), and density that costs performance (Where Winds Meet's Kaifeng).
- No recent Three Kingdoms game appears to compete on period accuracy; Koei Tecmo keeps iconic designs (e.g. Lu Bu's helmet) for recognisability. Han-specific material detail is therefore open ground for differentiation.

- Dynasty Warriors: Origins: no art-director statement on armour, banners or horses found; Intel's article said to describe crowds, costumes, banners and blood as deliberate spectacle returned 403.

I could not tie each claim to a verified URL, so treat them as leads only.

## 3. What Eastern Han / Three Kingdoms armour, tack, weapons, walls, gates, roofs, colours and banners actually looked like (versus the later look games borrow)

### Takeaway
Around 200 AD the evidence points to black-lacquered iron or leather lamellar covering mainly the torso, hood-like helmets for better-equipped troops, ring-pommel sabres, spears and 卜-type ji halberds, riders without riding stirrups, rammed-earth city walls with timber gate towers and paired que, and tiled timber buildings with simple cantilevered brackets — not mountain-pattern armour, guandao/crescent halberds, brick-faced walls with Ming barbicans, or yellow-tile-and-red-wall palaces. Three Kingdoms equipment is poorly attested directly (Cao Wei tombs were looted), so reconstructions lean on Eastern Han and Western Jin evidence.

### Cited Findings

Armour and helmets
- [REF] Han infantry wore lacquered rawhide, hardened lacquered leather, or iron/steel lamellar, with caps or iron helmets; the basic design continued from Qin. One Western Han iron suit had 328 lamellae. — [Wikipedia: Chinese armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_armour)
- [REVIEW/essay] Iron lamellar (铁札甲) became standard in the Han; plates were lacquered black; lamellar stayed in use until cloth-faced brigandine took over in Ming–Qing. — [gcores essay](https://www.gcores.com/articles/146210)
- [REVIEW/essay] Ordinary Han soldiers' armour protected only chest and abdomen; a helmet plus armour was high-grade kit whose lacing and lacquering were too laborious for mass issue; Three Kingdoms equipment "should basically be a continuation of Eastern Han", and because Cao Wei great tombs were looted and murals lost, reconstruction "can only lean toward Eastern Han and guess". — [gcores, 中国古代军戎服饰小论 ch. 5](https://www.gcores.com/articles/133305)
- [REF] Three Kingdoms texts: "dark armour" (xuan kai/xuan jia) — 5,000 suits captured in a 231 report, said to reflect sunlight; Cao Zhi lists "black-brilliant" (heiguang), "bright-brilliant" (mingguang) and "double-faced" (liangdang) suits; brilliant armour was decarburised steel that "shines blue-black when polished". — [Wikipedia: Chinese armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_armour); [Wikipedia: Military history of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Three_Kingdoms)
- [REF] A sleeved lamellar coat, tongxiu kai (筒袖铠), is attested for the Han; [HOBBY] its tube sleeves protect the armpit, and it was probably not issued in large numbers. — [Wikipedia: Chinese armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_armour); [Dragon's Armory: Han armor](https://dragonsarmory.blogspot.com/2016/08/han-armor.html)
- [REF] Helmets of the period take a hood-like shape with a hanging neck guard and no face protection. — [World History Encyclopedia (snippet)](https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1143/armour-in-ancient-chinese-warfare/)
- [HOBBY] Ground infantry often wore leather caps; mid-to-late Han commanders had scale aventails covering neck and chin; pauldrons lengthened; pom-poms on helmets are correct for later Eastern Han, Jin and Northern dynasties (from figurines). — [Dragon's Armory](https://dragonsarmory.blogspot.com/2016/08/han-armor.html)
- [REF] Typical lamellae are 4–10 cm wide (Bai Rongjin, CASS); the Marquis of Haihun's (Western Han) armour used about 6,000 composite scales of iron, copper and leather bonded with lacquer, each only about 1 cm wide and 0.2 cm thick — the smallest Han scales found. — [SCMP](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/3289874/tomb-chinese-emperor-liu-he-gives-han-dynasty-battle-secrets-armour-find); [Archaeology magazine](https://archaeology.org/?p=46540)
- [REF] Anachronisms for c. 200 AD: mountain-pattern armour (named in Tang texts, known from Song–Ming art, no surviving examples); brigandine (Ming); "cord and plaque" breastplates (420–589); fully comprehensive metal horse armour (mid-4th century, north-east China). Mail is mentioned by Cao Zhi but was rare and foreign. — [Wikipedia: Chinese armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_armour)
- [REVIEW/essay] Film errors called out: Wei helmets in 见龙卸甲 (2008) modelled on the Japanese Hineno kabuto, Shu "flying saucer" helmets like WWI British helmets; (snippet) the 1994 TV series gave Zhou Yu Japanese-style armour, while Red Cliff's arms are judged relatively good. — [gcores](https://www.gcores.com/articles/146210); [gcores (snippet)](https://www.gcores.com/articles/125825)

Shields and weapons
- [REF] "Great shields" protected front-line spearmen and crossbowmen; cavalry paired a shield with the single-edged dao; a small iron "hook shield" with hooks for catching polearms was used with a sword. — [Wikipedia: Chinese armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_armour)
- [HOBBY] Shields were wood, often with metal centre and rim, some lacquered with an iron rib. — [Dragon's Armory](https://dragonsarmory.blogspot.com/2016/08/han-armor.html)
- [REF] By the end of the period the single-edged dao had overtaken the straight double-edged jian as the main close-combat weapon; long spears and lances rose over halberds; crossbows faded after the Han. — [Wikipedia: Military history of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Three_Kingdoms)
- [HOBBY] Eastern Han ring-pommel dao (环首刀), with gilded bird/phoenix pommels for ranking officers. — [Dragon's Armory](https://dragonsarmory.blogspot.com/2016/08/han-armor.html)
- [REF] Han iron swords were roughly 1 m long (versus 0.5 m Warring States bronze); iron spears and iron ji replaced the bronze ge; arrowheads became all-iron by the end of the Han; the small trigger crossbow was the most common missile weapon. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)
- [REF] The Han ji is a spear/dagger-axe hybrid used by infantry, cavalry and charioteers. The crescent-bladed qinglong ji and fangtian ji are Song-dynasty forms (first evidence: an 11th-century Wujing Zongyao illustration); Lü Bu's fangtian ji and Guan Yu's guandao are anachronisms from the novel. — [Wikipedia: Ji (polearm)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ji_(polearm))
- [REVIEW/essay] Guan Yu more plausibly used a rigid-shafted spear or shuo; flexible waxwood shafts are a Ming–Qing civilian practice. — [gcores](https://www.gcores.com/articles/133305)
- [REF] Siege and field devices: traction trebuchets used by attackers and defenders; caltrops; wooden towers used by Yuan Shao at Guandu to shoot into Cao Cao's camp, answered with shields held overhead; battering rams. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty); [Wikipedia: Military history of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Three_Kingdoms)

Cavalry, tack and the stirrup question
- [REF] The earliest dated depiction is a single mounting stirrup on a Western Jin figurine from Changsha, 302 AD — on one side only and too short to ride in; the earliest reliable paired riding stirrups are on a figurine from an Eastern Jin tomb near Nanjing, c. 322; the earliest surviving pair is from Feng Sufu's tomb (415). A 1981 suggestion of Han stirrups from horse plaques was rebutted in 1984 (the horses had no saddles). — [Wikipedia: Stirrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirrup)
- [PRESS] A glazed pottery horseman from the Ding Feng family tomb in Nanjing (Ding Feng died 271, Eastern Wu) shows a triangular single stirrup on the left of a high-pommel saddle, pushing the single mounting stirrup back about 31 years into the Three Kingdoms; reported in early 2025. — [Guancha, 15 Jan 2025 (snippet)](https://www.guancha.cn/EErJin/2025_01_15_762093.shtml); [China News, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/dxw/2026/02-23/10575443.shtml)
- [PRESS] CASS zooarchaeologist Lü Peng: the evidence chain shows the move from single stirrup (mounting aid) to paired stirrups (stable riding) was completed across the Three Kingdoms–Wei–Jin period. Media phrasing about "rewriting military history" is journalistic. — [China News, 23 Feb 2026](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/dxw/2026/02-23/10575443.shtml)
- [REF] "Full riding stirrups would not appear until the 4th century." — [Wikipedia: Military history of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Three_Kingdoms)
- [REF, scholarly] The standard recent Chinese treatment is Chen Zhenyu et al., "再论马镫起源", Kaogu 2021.11 (English version in Chinese Archaeology). — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/char-2023-0012/pdf)
- [REF] Horse armour: wider and fuller horse armour is not attested until the late 2nd century; Cao Cao said he faced 300 sets with only ten of his own, possibly partial frontal barding; a 13 BC armoury inventory lists 5,330 horse armours. — [Wikipedia: Chinese armour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_armour)
- [REF] Harness: the Han replaced the chest yoke with a softer breast strap; the horse collar is a 5th-century development. Wheelbarrows appear in 2nd-century tomb murals. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)
- [REF] Gongsun Zan's White Cavalry: "armour and flags shined with brilliance" at Jie Bridge. — [Wikipedia: Military history of the Three Kingdoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_history_of_the_Three_Kingdoms)

Walls, gates, que and buildings
- [REF] Surviving Han architecture is brick, rammed earth, stone and ceramic tile; timber buildings did not survive (oldest timber halls are Tang). Brick and mud walls were not load-bearing; wooden pillars carried the roof. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)
- [REF] Eastern Han Luoyang's rammed-earth walls survive to about 10 m high and 25 m wide at the base; twelve gatehouses, three per side; Chang'an's gates each had three gateways about 6 m wide; Chang'an's moat was 8 m wide and 3 m deep. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)
- [REF] Rammed earth is compacted in layers within formwork; Han forts in Xinjiang interleave brushwood/poplar with tamped earth; most Chinese walls were sloped, not vertical; gate towers of two or three storeys, mainly timber, sat on a widened section of wall. Covering rammed earth with an outer skin of brick or stone is attributed to the Song and later; the archery-tower barbican reached its final form in Ming–Qing; the brick-and-stone Great Wall is a Ming rebuild. — [Wikipedia: Chinese city wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_city_wall); [Wikipedia: Chinese architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_architecture)
- [REF] Frontier: rammed-earth Great Wall sections in Gansu with beacon towers and two crenellated forts. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)
- [REF] Que (阙): freestanding ceremonial gate towers set in pairs before palace, temple, government and tomb entrances, at their peak in the Han; about 30 stone examples survive (mostly Sichuan, e.g. Gao Yi's que at Ya'an, 6 m tall), carved to imitate timber buildings with tiled eaves. Han tomb reliefs (e.g. Yinan, Shandong) show buildings fronted by que. They later fused into U-shaped gates such as the Ming Meridian Gate. — [Wikipedia: Que (tower)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Que_(tower))
- [REF] Roofs were thatch or tile; tiled eaves projected to keep rain off the walls and were carried on dougong, some elaborately decorated; tile ends were round with moulded designs, matching excavated examples from Chang'an and Luoyang. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)
- [REF] The Met's Eastern Han "Central Watchtower" (green lead-glazed earthenware, 104.1 cm) stands on four corner piers with a widely overhanging tile roof "held up by cantilevered brackets", a second roof over the first storey, an exterior stair, lattice screens and a circular window. — [Met Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/49519); [AMICO description](https://amica.davidrumsey.com/amico1280850-104924.html)
- [REF] Pottery models show walled lower courtyards, balconies with balustrades, door knockers, granaries on stilts with tiled roofs and brackets, mills, wells with pulley roofs; houses had courtyards with elevated halls, plastered and painted walls and stairways. A Henan model shows a fortified multi-storey manor with a covered bridge to a smaller watchtower. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty); [Archaeology magazine, "Pigpens and Penthouses"](https://archaeology.org/?p=55840)
- [REF] Brackets grew into complex interlocking sets by Tang–Song; in the Ming they "became smaller and more numerous" and more decorative — i.e. dense rows of small brackets are a late look. — [Wikipedia: Dougong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dougong)
- [REF] Yellow tiles with red walls (Forbidden City) date to the Ming Yongle era. — [Wikipedia: Chinese architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_architecture)
- [REF] Bridges were mostly timber beam-on-trestle; pontoon bridges of chained boats crossed the Yellow and Yangzi rivers for military use; ships had stern rudders, flat bottoms and square ends. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty)

Colours, banners and standards
- [REF, scholarly] Michael Loewe: the Han founder was associated with Red, "the colour of Fire", in both Shiji and Hanshu; a later view (tied to Wang Mang, Gongsun Shu and Guangwu) held that Fire was the dynasty's proper element. — [Loewe, "Water, Earth and Fire – the Symbols of the Han Dynasty" (1979)](https://www.oag.uni-hamburg.de/noag-archiv/noag-125-1979/loewe.pdf)
- [REF] Pigments in Later Han tomb murals (Beijing): carbon black, calcite white, cinnabar red; cinnabar is the most common Han mural red. — [Heritage Science, 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s40494-023-01056-6)
- [REF] Early Chinese flags were typically tied to a long pole, sometimes with a small horizontal bar, and made of hide, feathers or fabric painted with patterns or colours. Liu Xi's Eastern Han dictionary Shiming says the bear-and-tiger banner (旗) is what army commanders set up, "like fierce tigers". The Zhouli lists nine banner types (sun-and-moon, dragons, plain silk, mixed silk, bear-and-tiger, bird/falcon, turtle-and-snake, whole feathers, split feathers); the Mozi describes signalling with five coloured flags (blue-green, red, yellow, white, black). — [Ji Ruxun, Proc. 30th Int. Congress of Vexillology, 2024](https://fiav.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/ICV30-15-Ji-Tracing-the-Origin.pdf)
- [REF] Warring States inlaid bronzes show two battle-flag types: a long cloth strip with several round discs, and paired long pheasant-tail streamers; an early Eastern Han chariot-procession tomb mural (Anping) shows four riders carrying small flags, apparently signalling; early Western Han T-shaped silk banners from Changsha hang from a cross-bar; in the Luoshen fu scroll (Eastern Jin, Song copy) carriage standards are yak/ox-tail tufts (旄) and long streamers. — [Ji Ruxun, ICV30 paper](https://fiav.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/ICV30-15-Ji-Tracing-the-Origin.pdf)

Primary visual references named by sources
- Wu Family (Wu Liang) shrines, Jiaxiang, Shandong, 151 AD — extensive carved reliefs. — [Wikipedia: Wu Family Shrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wu_Family_Shrines)
- Leitai tomb, Wuwei, c. 200 AD — over 200 bronze figurines of men, horses and chariots (the "Flying Horse" tomb), exactly the game's period. — [Wikipedia: Flying Horse of Gansu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flying_Horse_of_Gansu)
- Yangjiawan (Western Han) — about 3,000 cavalry statuettes c. 60 cm, plus foot soldiers. — [Wikipedia: Yangjiawan terracotta army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yangjiawan_terracotta_army)
- Eastern Han pottery towers and manors (Met; a British Museum watchtower 86 cm high). — [Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/49519); [World History Encyclopedia image entry](https://www.worldhistory.org/image/7528/han-watchtower-model/)
- Yinan tomb reliefs (buildings with que); Sichuan stone que. — [Wikipedia: Que (tower)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Que_(tower))
- For texts and people: Rafe de Crespigny's Biographical Dictionary of Later Han to the Three Kingdoms (used by Creative Assembly). — [Total War blog](https://www.totalwar.com/?p=8457)

### Inferences
- A defensible "c. 200 AD" checklist for procedural models: (a) torso-only lamellar on rank and file, sleeved/skirted lamellar and hooded helmets only on officers and guards; (b) armour dark — black-lacquered or blue-black polished iron — which happens to match the game's "blackened iron" direction; (c) riders with saddles but no riding stirrups (at most a single left-side mounting loop for late-period Wu figures); legs hang or grip; (d) weapons: ring-pommel straight sabre, spear/lance, 卜-shaped ji, crossbow, large shields — no guandao, no crescent halberd; (e) walls: battered rammed earth with visible horizontal ramming layers, no brick skin, no Ming barbican; timber gate tower of two or three storeys on top; paired que flanking important gates; (f) buildings: timber frame on a rammed-earth platform, plastered walls, grey tile with round tile-ends, wide eaves on a few large simple brackets rather than dense small ones; multi-storey watchtowers with balconies; (g) banners: pole-mounted cloth strips, streamers, feather or yak-tail tufts, animal emblems (bear/tiger); red is the safest Han state colour.
- Because direct Three Kingdoms evidence is thin, "Eastern Han plus early Western Jin" is the legitimate envelope; anything Tang or later (mingguang chest discs in their Tang form, mountain pattern, full cataphract barding) is a choice, not history.
- The horse-armour evidence allows a few partially barded elite horses by c. 200 but not full cataphracts.

### Gaps
- Roof profile: I could not source the commonly stated point that Han roofs were essentially straight-pitched with little or no upturned eave (curved, flying eaves being later). The Wikipedia pages read do not date the change. Needs a source (e.g. Liu Xujie's Qin–Han chapter in Steinhardt, ed., *Chinese Architecture*, Yale 2002).
- Bracket forms (一斗二升 / 一斗三升), tile-end motifs (cloud patterns, auspicious characters, four spirits) and whether Han city walls had crenellations, corner towers or projecting bastions: not sourced.
- Uniform colours: I found no source read in this session for robe colours of Han soldiers (the Yangjiawan page does not describe its paint). The "Han troops wore red" convention is unverified here.
- Eastern Han helmet and "pen ling" (盆领) high-collar details, lacing colour (red cord), and exact plate shapes: not sourced; Albert Dien's "A Study of Early Chinese Armor" (1981) is the standard reference but was not accessible.
- Banners: no source for Three Kingdoms-specific standards (牙旗 commander's flag, 麾, 幢, the tufted 节 staff) or their colours; five-colour directional army flags are attested in theory texts but one museum popular article applying them to armies gave no citation.
- The Ji Ruxun paper places the Anping mural tomb in "Shaanxi"; I believe the Anping Eastern Han mural tomb is in Hebei — unverified, flag before repeating.
- Props (unverified leads only): pole drums and bells as command signals, bronze lamps and incense burners, braziers, ox carts and two-wheeled chariots/carriages in processions, well-heads with pulleys (the last is sourced above). Helinger (Inner Mongolia) murals of walled towns were not checked.
- Dragon's Armory is a hobbyist blog and partly relies on film stills; its claims are marked [HOBBY] and should not carry weight alone.

## 4. Colour and lighting scripts — grading dark, mythic scenes while keeping characters readable

### Takeaway
Developer specifics are scarce outside Sucker Punch: the documented techniques are art-directed light placement (god-ray targets, cloud holes), fog used as a separating layer with one saturated accent, refusal of blanket haze/colour filters, and "clear silhouettes that read instantly". For genuinely dark games the evidence is mostly from critics, who reward hand-tuned lighting and punish muddy darkness, inconsistent exposure and effects that overpower the scene.

### Cited Findings
- [DEV] Sucker Punch lets artists place "god ray targets" so particular areas are lit more often through gaps in the clouds; a cloud shadow map makes shafts visible in volumetric fog at distance. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [DEV] Co-director Jason Connell: adding haze or coloured filters would "crush" natural colour — "The vibrancy of the local vegetation would be dulled"; the team borrowed a photography approach to keep light as clear as possible (reporter's paraphrase). — [Atmos](https://atmos.earth/art-and-culture/this-years-most-anticipated-video-game-is-an-homage-and-elegy-to-nature/)
- [DEV] Joanna Wang on a dark, smoky set piece (Matsumae Castle): "The air is thick with smoke and fire, visibility drops low"; on an accent through fog: "vibrant red leaves that almost glow through the mist"; on a distant lure: "A single red tree burning on a distant hill". — [VFX Voice](https://vfxvoice.com/video-games-ghost-of-yotei/)
- [DEV] Wang: "Clear silhouettes that read instantly"; "The important things can stand out – your path, your goal and the emotional beat of the moment"; "color became a way to elevate the narrative tone"; "Each region has its own moment in the year". — [VFX Voice](https://vfxvoice.com/video-games-ghost-of-yotei/)
- [DEV] Wang on a single recurring accent hue: a striking yellow ginkgo, "giving the player a hint of that yellow to remind the player of her story"; composition by colour massing: "We painted orange-yellow trees around all of the hills and left the golden field in the middle". — [MobileSyrup](https://mobilesyrup.com/2025/09/25/ghost-of-yotei-sucker-punch-joanna-wang-art-director-interview/)
- [DEV] (snippet) Wang describes art direction as "the skin of the world", says light is as important as dialogue, and treats dynamic skies and weather as emotional tools rather than tech features. — [Domus](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2025/09/30/ghost-of-yotei-videogame.html)
- [DEV] Yōtei uses baked lighting plus short-range ray-traced GI; AC Shadows uses ray-traced GI specifically so lighting responds when lamps are switched and props destroyed. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201); [Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/2NvGq2f2HRuu11c03KJ0DG/assassins-creed-shadows-building-the-next-generation-of-assassins-creed)
- [REVIEW] Black Myth: Wukong's hand-authored (non-path-traced) lighting is good enough that full ray tracing can look disappointing beside it. — [DSOGaming](https://www.dsogaming.com/?p=182365)
- [REVIEW] Zhong Kui's 2026 trailer: drab grey palette, little greenery. — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/see-what-black-myth-zhong-kui-brings-in-15-minute-gameplay-trailer)
- [REVIEW] Failure pattern: "dark and muddy lighting contrasted with retina-burning combat effects" (Nioh series history); Nioh 3 improved by being "cleaner and easier to parse", yet lighting still swings between scenes and some areas are too dark. — [COGconnected](https://cogconnected.com/review/nioh-3-review/); [Prima Games](https://primagames.com/reviews/nioh-3-review)
- [REVIEW] Wuchang is framed as bringing colour to a muted genre, but its two visual registers clash without a unifying treatment. — [Epic Games Store](https://store.epicgames.com/news/wuchang-fallen-feathers-brings-color-to-the-muted-soulslike-genre); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/rpg/wuchang-fallen-feathers-review)
- [DEV, 2019] Total War: Three Kingdoms costume design balanced realism against readability at distance. — [Total War blog](https://www.totalwar.com/?p=12572) (search excerpt)
- [REF] Period palette anchors: carbon black, calcite white and cinnabar red are the dominant Later Han mural pigments. — [Heritage Science, 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s40494-023-01056-6)

### Inferences
- A workable script for a cold, low-key "dark myth" scene, assembled from the above: keep global light clear rather than washing the frame with a tinted haze; use fog as a depth separator between layers; reserve saturation for one accent family (the game's fire/bronze-gold; historically, cinnabar red and black lacquer are period-true accents); steer attention with placed light pools; and give characters silhouettes that survive against fog. This is my synthesis; no single source states it as a rule.
- The critic evidence implies that consistency of exposure across scenes, and effects brightness matched to scene brightness, matter more to perceived quality than extra darkness.
- Readability at distance (Total War) is the constraint most relevant to the campaign map and war theatre; Sucker Punch's silhouette rule is most relevant to the duel.

### Gaps
- No numeric or semi-numeric specifics found for any title: key-to-fill ratios, fog colour values, rim-light practice, LUT/grade descriptions, or time-of-day policies. No art-book lighting pages were accessible.
- No Game Science, Leenzee, S-Game, Everstone or Koei Tecmo statement on colour grading or lighting keys was found.

## 5. Detail density — where these games concentrate detail and where they rest the eye

### Takeaway
The clearest statement is Sucker Punch's: "we're constantly simplifying, stripping away anything that doesn't matter" so that path, goal and emotional beat stand out, with large spaces made "intentional and emotional, not empty" through layered landmarks and a single strong accent. Elsewhere the pattern is visible mainly through reception: detail is concentrated in scanned hero objects (statues, temples) and showpiece cities, and reviewers penalise density that buys nothing (performance loss, clutter blocking the action, repeated sets).

### Cited Findings
- [DEV] Joanna Wang: "we're constantly simplifying, stripping away anything that doesn't matter"; "We had to make those massive spaces feel intentional and emotional, not empty." — [VFX Voice](https://vfxvoice.com/video-games-ghost-of-yotei/)
- [DEV] Wang's layered-landmark example: "The pagoda stands behind a foggy bamboo forest, a wind-shaped tree in the middle of the hill" — foreground mass, mid-ground character object, background landmark. — [VFX Voice](https://vfxvoice.com/video-games-ghost-of-yotei/)
- [DEV] Wang: emotion in "even a single leaf", amplified by repeating it across a very large field — i.e. one carefully designed small element, instanced, rather than many different ones. — [Atmos](https://atmos.earth/art-and-culture/this-years-most-anticipated-video-game-is-an-homage-and-elegy-to-nature/)
- [DEV] (snippet) Sucker Punch used contrast between tight, confined spaces and wide fields to make the world feel bigger without enlarging it; the team studied how grass is painted in classical Japanese works to capture shape and movement. — [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/?p=639154); [Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/?p=52211)
- [DEV] Distant detail is budgeted hard: a million-plus far objects reduced to about 60,000 drawn; far mountains are baked. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=411201)
- [PRESS] Black Myth: Wukong's scanning effort targeted temples, Buddha/deity statues, rocks and forests — the statues being the most-cited recognisable detail (Yuhuang Temple's Twenty-Eight Mansions). — [21st Century Business Herald](https://m.21jingji.com/article/20240908/herald/ef6d0bc54aa528204297d7bd0ec91d88.html); [CGTN](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-03-02/From-digital-pixels-to-700-year-old-clay-1LbdSsBXw7m/share_amp.html)
- [REVIEW] A gcores author credits Game Science with avoiding "detail hell" (author's opinion). — [gcores](https://www.gcores.com/articles/187308)
- [REVIEW] Where Winds Meet concentrates density in Kaifeng, which drew the strongest praise and the worst performance complaints; one critic says its excess holds it back. — [Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/review/where-winds-meet-review-living-the-wuxia-dream/); [GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/where-winds-meet-is-a-graphics-masterpiece-but-kaifeng-has-severe-performance-issues); [Military.com](https://365.military.com/off-duty/games/where-winds-meet-review-massive-game-struggles-under-its-own-weight.html)
- [DEV] Where Winds Meet's concept team: "We attach great importance to characters, people, and environment" in scene composition. — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/gaming/where-winds-meet-interview)
- [REVIEW] Phantom Blade Zero: dense environments sometimes obscure finishing-move framing. — [Creative Bloq](https://creativebloq.com/reviews/phantom-blade-zero-review-dark-wuxia-martial-arts-masters-with-an-apprentices-execution)
- [REVIEW] Wuchang: environments outshine character models (detail weighted to the world, not the figure). — [GamingBible](https://www.gamingbible.com/reviews/wuchang-fallen-feathers-review-337450-20250722)
- [REVIEW] Dynasty Warriors: Origins puts its spectacle into massed "Large Forces" while reusing battlefield sets, which reviewers noticed. — [Gamer Guides](https://www.gamerguides.com/dynasty-warriors-origins/reviews/review); [Noisy Pixel](https://noisypixel.net/dynasty-warriors-origins-review/)
- [MARKETING] 率土之滨's 3D map advertises courtyards visible inside cities — detail spent on city interiors at map scale. — [Xiaomi Game Center article](https://game.xiaomi.com/viewpoint/1098536009_1537942179919_16)
- [REF] Han craftsmen's own models put detail on roof edges (tile-ends), brackets, balconies/balustrades, lattice windows, door knockers and small figures, leaving wall planes plain. — [Wikipedia: Science and technology of the Han dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_of_the_Han_dynasty); [AMICO description of the Met watchtower](https://amica.davidrumsey.com/amico1280850-104924.html)

### Inferences
- A density rule consistent with these sources: spend detail on (1) silhouettes and edges — roof eaves, tile-ends, brackets, battlements, banner tips, helmet crests; (2) one hero object per view — a gate tower with paired que, a general, a standard; (3) a single well-designed small element instanced widely (lamellar plate rows, grass blades, tile courses); and leave wall planes, rammed-earth faces, ground and fog-filled distance quiet.
- The Han pottery models are a ready-made period guide to this hierarchy: plain walls, busy eaves and balconies.
- For the war theatre, Dynasty Warriors: Origins suggests that mass (many simple figures with banners) reads as richness more than per-figure detail; for the city diorama, Where Winds Meet warns that uniform density costs frame rate and legibility.

### Gaps
- No conference talk or art-book text from any of these studios explicitly discussing focal detail versus rest areas was found for 2024–2026 beyond the Sucker Punch interviews.
- No developer statement on detail allocation for strategy-map scale (campaign maps, city icons) from Koei Tecmo, Creative Assembly or the Chinese SLG studios was found.
- The gcores "detail hell" remark is one author's opinion, not evidence of Game Science's process.
